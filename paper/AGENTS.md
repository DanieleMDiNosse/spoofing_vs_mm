# Scientific Paper Writing Instructions

## Primary objective

Write scientific prose that is rigorous, precise, and easy to understand on the first reading.

Preserve the full conceptual, mathematical, statistical, and methodological complexity of the paper. Simplify the exposition, not the scientific content.

Clarity has priority over sounding sophisticated. Do not make sentences more complex merely to imitate conventional academic writing.

## Target reader

Assume that the reader is a technically competent researcher who understands the general field but is not familiar with the specific model, notation, dataset, assumptions, or experimental design used in this paper.

Do not explain standard background concepts unnecessarily. However, explain all paper-specific definitions, mechanisms, assumptions, and modelling choices clearly.

## Core writing principles

* Preserve all scientific claims, qualifications, assumptions, and limitations.
* Use direct and precise academic English.
* Express one main claim per sentence whenever possible.
* Unfold multi-step reasoning sequentially.
* Prefer two clear sentences over one dense sentence.
* Put the main statement before secondary qualifications.
* Make causal, contrastive, and inferential relations explicit.
* Use technical terminology when it is standard and necessary.
* Do not add technical terminology merely to sound more formal.
* Do not replace precise concepts with vague or generic language.
* Do not introduce claims that are not supported by the manuscript, results, or provided sources.

## Sentence structure

Construct sentences so that the reader can identify the subject, action, and main claim without rereading.

In particular:

* Keep the subject close to the main verb.
* Avoid deeply nested subordinate clauses.
* Avoid inserting long qualifications between the subject and the verb.
* Avoid sentences containing several independent claims.
* Avoid long sequences of prepositional phrases.
* Avoid stacking multiple contrasts, causes, and qualifications in the same sentence.
* Split a sentence when it contains several logical steps.
* Use semicolons sparingly.
* Use parentheses only for secondary information.
* Do not hide an important claim inside parentheses or a subordinate clause.

Sentence length is not a rigid constraint. Longer sentences are acceptable when they remain linear and easy to parse. The goal is low syntactic complexity, not uniformly short prose.

## Sequential reasoning

Do not compress a multi-step argument into one sentence merely because the steps are logically related.

For example, when explaining a mechanism, present the steps in order:

1. state the relevant assumption or input;
2. explain the immediate mechanism;
3. describe the resulting effect;
4. interpret the implication.

Each step should normally receive its own sentence when combining them would make the argument harder to follow.

Preserve conceptual depth, but unfold the reasoning sequentially rather than compressing it syntactically.

## Paragraph structure

Each paragraph should have one clear rhetorical purpose.

A paragraph should normally:

1. state its main point;
2. explain the mechanism, reasoning, or evidence;
3. add necessary technical details or qualifications;
4. state the implication or connect to the next paragraph.

The first sentence should usually reveal the purpose of the paragraph.

Do not mix several distinct functions in one paragraph unless their connection is immediate. In particular, avoid combining all of the following in the same paragraph:

* methodological description;
* literature comparison;
* presentation of results;
* interpretation of results;
* limitations;
* policy or practical implications.

Separate these functions when doing so improves the logical structure.

## Logical connections

Make the relationship between consecutive sentences explicit.

Use appropriate connectors when the second sentence provides:

* a cause;
* a consequence;
* a contrast;
* a qualification;
* an example;
* an interpretation;
* a comparison;
* a limitation.

Suitable connectors include:

* because;
* therefore;
* as a result;
* however;
* by contrast;
* whereas;
* although;
* nevertheless;
* similarly;
* unlike;
* in particular;
* under this assumption;
* this occurs because;
* this result suggests.

Do not insert connectors mechanically. Use them only when they accurately describe the logical relation.

## Vocabulary and tone

Use a formal scientific tone without unnecessary abstraction or inflated language.

Prefer direct formulations such as:

* “use” rather than “make use of”;
* “show” rather than “provide evidence for the presence of”;
* “because” rather than “due to the fact that”;
* “to” rather than “in order to”, unless emphasis is needed;
* “we estimate” rather than “an estimation is performed”;
* “the model assumes” rather than “the model is based on the assumption that”;
* “changes” rather than “induces a modification in”;
* “measures” rather than “provides a measurement of”.

Avoid:

* academic filler;
* decorative complexity;
* vague claims;
* excessive hedging;
* unnecessary repetition;
* inflated expressions;
* overly promotional language;
* claims such as “novel”, “important”, “significant”, or “remarkable” unless they are justified precisely.

Do not use words such as “fundamental”, “crucial”, “comprehensive”, “robust”, or “innovative” as generic intensifiers.

## Verbs and nominalizations

Prefer concrete verbs to unnecessary nominalizations.

For example:

* prefer “we estimate the parameter” to “we perform the estimation of the parameter”;
* prefer “the mechanism changes liquidity” to “the mechanism induces a change in liquidity”;
* prefer “heterogeneous agents differ in latency” to “the introduction of agent heterogeneity allows for the representation of differences in latency”.

Nominalizations are acceptable when they are standard technical terms or when the noun itself is the object of discussion.

## Active and passive voice

Prefer active voice when it makes the actor and action clearer.

Use passive voice when:

* the actor is irrelevant;
* the procedure or result is the natural focus;
* active voice would produce an awkward or repetitive sentence.

Do not use passive voice merely to sound more academic.

## Technical precision

Never improve readability by weakening scientific precision.

In particular:

* preserve distinctions between correlation and causation;
* preserve distinctions between assumptions, definitions, results, and interpretations;
* preserve uncertainty and statistical qualifications;
* preserve the scope under which a result holds;
* preserve necessary conditions and exceptions;
* preserve differences between theoretical predictions and empirical findings;
* preserve distinctions between calibration, estimation, simulation, and validation;
* preserve distinctions between descriptive, predictive, and causal claims.

Do not replace a precise technical term with a simpler but inaccurate synonym.

## Mathematical exposition

Before an equation:

* explain why the equation is introduced;
* state what object or mechanism it defines;
* clarify the role it plays in the argument.

After an equation:

* explain the meaning of the main terms;
* describe the economic, statistical, mathematical, or physical interpretation;
* state the implication of the equation when relevant.

Do not merely repeat the equation in words.

Clearly distinguish among:

* definitions;
* assumptions;
* identities;
* estimators;
* model equations;
* derived results;
* empirical specifications;
* interpretations.

When a derivation involves several conceptual steps, explain the steps in order rather than compressing the full derivation into one sentence.

## Methods sections

When describing a method:

1. state its purpose;
2. define its inputs;
3. explain the mechanism;
4. specify its outputs;
5. clarify the assumptions;
6. explain how it is implemented or estimated;
7. state how it is evaluated.

Do not present implementation details before the reader understands what the method is intended to do.

## Results sections

When presenting a result:

1. state the main empirical or numerical finding;
2. identify the relevant comparison or baseline;
3. provide the supporting quantity, figure, or table;
4. explain the mechanism only when supported;
5. distinguish the result from its interpretation;
6. state relevant limitations or scope conditions.

Do not force the reader to infer the principal result from a list of numbers.

Avoid repeating every value from a table or figure. Highlight the values that support the argument.

## Literature review

When discussing previous work:

* explain why each cited contribution is relevant;
* distinguish clearly between similarities and differences;
* avoid citation lists without interpretation;
* do not exaggerate novelty by understating related work;
* state the paper’s contribution in concrete terms;
* avoid vague formulations such as “the literature has largely overlooked this issue” unless this claim is well supported.

Prefer comparisons based on specific dimensions, such as:

* assumptions;
* market structure;
* data;
* identification strategy;
* agent behaviour;
* objective function;
* equilibrium concept;
* estimation method;
* evaluation criterion.

## Contributions

State contributions precisely and concretely.

A contribution statement should specify:

* what is introduced or analysed;
* how it differs from existing approaches;
* what new result, capability, or evidence it provides;
* under which setting the contribution applies.

Avoid claiming that the paper is the “first” to do something unless this has been carefully verified.

## LaTeX and notation

When editing LaTeX:

* preserve all commands, labels, references, citations, equations, and environments;
* do not alter mathematical notation unless explicitly requested;
* do not rename variables without checking consistency throughout the project;
* preserve `\cite`, `\ref`, `\eqref`, `\label`, and bibliography keys;
* preserve custom macros;
* do not convert valid LaTeX notation into plain text;
* do not modify equation numbering or cross-references unnecessarily;
* keep terminology and notation consistent across sections.

Before introducing a new symbol, verify whether an equivalent symbol already exists in the manuscript.

## Editing existing text

When rewriting existing passages:

* preserve the exact scientific meaning;
* preserve citations;
* preserve mathematical content;
* preserve necessary qualifications;
* preserve limitations;
* preserve the intended level of certainty;
* do not add new evidence;
* do not invent interpretations;
* do not remove inconvenient details;
* do not summarize unless explicitly requested;
* do not make the prose more assertive than the evidence allows.

The rewritten version should normally contain the same substantive information as the original, but with clearer syntax and logical organization.

## Avoiding typical LLM prose

Do not produce text with the following common LLM characteristics:

* unnecessarily long introductory clauses;
* repeated use of “Moreover”, “Furthermore”, and “Additionally”;
* excessive use of “This highlights”, “This underscores”, or “This emphasizes”;
* generic concluding sentences that add no information;
* repetitive statements of the same contribution;
* artificial three-item lists;
* frequent use of em dashes;
* vague references such as “this approach”, “this aspect”, or “this framework” when the antecedent is unclear;
* inflated phrases such as “a nuanced understanding”, “a comprehensive framework”, or “a multifaceted perspective”;
* paragraphs that restate rather than advance the argument.

Every sentence should perform a clear function.

## Readability revision

Before returning scientific prose, silently perform the following revision:

1. Identify the main claim of each sentence.
2. Check whether the sentence contains more than one main claim.
3. Split the sentence when the claims can be stated more clearly in sequence.
4. Move the main claim toward the beginning.
5. Reduce unnecessary subordinate clauses.
6. Replace unnecessary nominalizations with verbs.
7. Remove academic filler and redundant wording.
8. Check that pronouns have unambiguous antecedents.
9. Make implicit causal or contrastive relations explicit.
10. Check that each paragraph has one main purpose.
11. Verify that no scientific content has been removed or altered.
12. Verify that LaTeX syntax, citations, notation, and cross-references remain valid.

## Final quality criterion

The final text should satisfy both conditions:

1. A specialist should find it technically accurate and rigorous.
2. A researcher who is new to the specific paper should understand the argument without repeatedly rereading individual sentences.

When clarity and stylistic sophistication conflict, choose clarity. When clarity and technical precision appear to conflict, rewrite the explanation until both are preserved.
